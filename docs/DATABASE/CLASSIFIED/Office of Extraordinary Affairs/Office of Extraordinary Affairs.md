**Established:** 724  
**Organization Type:** Supranational Special Affairs Organization
**Origin:** Netherlands

The **Office of Extraordinary Affairs**, commonly abbreviated as the **OEA**, was established in 724 in the aftermath of **The Incursion**. Its creation was motivated by the desire to prevent another event of comparable scale from occurring, with the organization tasked with identifying and responding to threats considered capable of endangering humanity on a comparable level.

The OEA operates in the name of mankind itself and possesses the authority, by its own mandate, to disregard the sovereignty of individual governments when circumstances are deemed to require it. This authority is not universally recognized, however, and the organization's limited international presence has prevented it from consistently enforcing such claims.

Unlike most large organizations, the OEA possesses no centralized leadership. Instead, its operations are divided among independent headquarters, each of which is responsible for its own administration and operations. Despite their independence, all OEA headquarters operate according to a common set of directives defining the threats and activities against which the organization is authorized to act.

Membership in the OEA carries significant personal obligations. Every member is required to swear a formal vow and place a seal upon themselves, both serving as guarantees of loyalty to the organization's cause above personal, political, or national interests. The precise nature and function of these seals varies between accounts, though their existence is consistently reported across surviving OEA records.

Despite the scope of its mandate, the OEA has never become a widespread organization. Only a limited number of governments are aware of its existence, and fewer are willing to provide direct assistance. Government funding is therefore insufficient to sustain the organization on its own. OEA personnel frequently obtain necessary resources through the acquisition of supplies by force, mercenary contracts, and private contributions from wealthy benefactors. Such activities are permitted only when they do not conflict with the organization's established directives.

The OEA's importance has declined considerably over the centuries. Advances in technology, the gradual loss of widespread magical knowledge, and the weakening of humanity's connections to other planes have reduced both the number and severity of extraordinary threats requiring its intervention. These developments have also contributed to a persistent decline in OEA personnel and resources.

In the modern era, the OEA remains active, but its role has become considerably narrower than that for which it was originally established. In practice, many of its surviving operations now resemble conventional counter-terrorism and threat-response activities, with only occasional involvement in matters of a truly extraordinary nature.
