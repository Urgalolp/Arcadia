Gene splicing is a field of biotechnology believed to have originated during or shortly after **The Null**, and has remained an important part of human and medical advancement ever since. Through a combination of alchemy, magic, and advanced biological manipulation, it is possible to alter the genetic composition of a living individual by introducing or removing specific genes from their body.

Despite the capabilities of the technology, gene splicing has significant limitations. Existing genes cannot be directly altered to freely modify physical characteristics such as height, body structure, or skin coloration. Instead, splicing relies upon the introduction or removal of genetic material already capable of producing the desired biological traits.

One of the most significant consequences of gene splicing was its ability to bridge the biological distinctions between different races. By introducing compatible genetic material into an individual, traits previously exclusive to one lineage could be incorporated into another. This greatly expanded the biological diversity of the world's population and contributed to the emergence of individuals possessing characteristics associated with multiple races.

The technology also found widespread use for aesthetic purposes. Because multiple genetic sequences can be incorporated into an individual's existing genetic code, within certain biological limitations, it became possible to produce substantial changes in appearance without requiring extensive alterations to the remainder of the individual's genome. As a result, gene splicing came to serve not only medical and evolutionary purposes, but also cosmetic ones.

Gene splicing remains a physically demanding procedure. The older an individual is, the more difficult it becomes to successfully integrate new genetic material into their body. The procedure also carries a non-zero mortality rate, with the risk increasing according to both the individual's age and the complexity of the modification being attempted.

Successful splicing does not necessarily prevent an individual from reproducing. An individual who has undergone gene splicing can bear offspring, and any splicing present within their genetic lineage may potentially be inherited by those offspring, provided the relevant genetic material remains present within the parent's genome. This includes genes that are currently dormant or otherwise not expressed in the parent.
